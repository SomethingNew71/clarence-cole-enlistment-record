Chapter I

The record

Every morning of the war, the clerk of Battery C filled out a card. Who was present, who was sick, who had been promoted, and — in four or five handwritten lines under Record of Events — where the battery was and what it had done.

Those cards survive as 284 microfilm frames, covering the battalion from New York to the end of the war in Europe. Every dated statement on this site comes from one of them, and names the frame it came from. The long stretches where nothing changed — in position firing, day after day — are in the Archive, every card of them.

Chapter II

The soldier

His discharge record, box by box. He was a thirty-seven-year-old farmer from Tuscola County when he sailed, and thirty-nine when he came home.

The enlistment card

The Army made a punch card for every man it took in. His survives in the Archives, filmed on roll 1034. It was made four and a half years before the discharge above, and the two do not agree on when his service began: the card says 21 March 1941, the discharge says 27 November 1942. Both are shown as they read.

Chapter III

The crossing

The battery left Fort Slocum by harbor boat on the evening of 2 May 1944, boarded an Army transport at Terminal Pier at 2315, and cleared the New York Port of Embarkation at 0630 the next morning. The morning report does not name the destination — convoy destinations were not written on a form that travelled with the ship.

One hundred eighteen men were on the roll. One hundred seventeen were present for duty.

The destination is filled in eighteen months later, on his discharge: England, 15 May 1944. Twelve days at sea.

Chapter IV

The advance

A field artillery battalion moved often and rarely far: a few miles forward to a new firing position, then a few miles more. Plotted together, those moves follow the line of the advance.

Every position on the map is a village the battery named in its own station entry, joined in the order it reached them: Omaha Beach to the Cotentin, across France in the pursuit, into Belgium, and over the German frontier. The battery logged 979 road miles for itself in the process.

Five campaigns

Five campaign credits, carried as five bronze service stars on the European–African–Middle Eastern ribbon. The bars are the War Department's official dates for each campaign; the tinted band marks the period named in his Bronze Star citation.

Chapter V

The Bronze Star

The Bronze Star was established in 1944 for heroic or meritorious achievement in ground combat. Cole's award was for meritorious service: work done well over a long period, rather than a single act.

His citation describes ammunition supply. A battery could fire hundreds of rounds in a day. Someone had to know how many rounds of each type were on hand, bring more forward over roads under fire, and check that what arrived was undamaged. That was Cole's section.

Citation — Award of Bronze Star Medal

Sergeant Clarence J. Cole, 36106875, Field Artillery, Battery C 153d Field Artillery Battalion, United States Army, for meritorious service in support of active combat operations from 30 June 1944 to 15 March 1945 in France, Germany and Belgium. Sergeant Cole supplied the battery with its ammunition supply records, maintained the basic load at all times and also made continuous checks to ascertain that the ammunition was in no way damaged in transit. Showing marked qualities of leadership, Sergeant Cole has managed his section expertly and has performed his duties in an exemplary manner. Entered military service from Michigan.

Transcribed from the original in the family papers. See the document.

Still open

The general orders number and the date of the award appear on neither the citation nor the discharge. Awards were normally announced in the morning reports. The whole film has now been read, and no card announces it. The battalion's own general orders for 1943–45 survive at the National Archives at College Park, filed as FABN-153-1.13. They have not been read.

Chapter VI

Coming home

The war in Europe ended on 8 May 1945; the battery stayed on in Germany for the occupation. The first card found that names Sergeant Cole is dated 20 August 1945: five days' temporary duty at Treebeek, in Dutch Limburg — a leave, four months after the war ended. Five days later his entry reads Fr TD to dy: returned to duty.

That same week the battalion was being reorganised. On 24 August, Special Orders 66 transferred 142 men with enough points to the 70th Infantry Division for return to the United States. Cole had 80 points — within the range of the men sent home — but he is not on the order. The Battalion room maps every man the two orders name.

He left Battery C on 5 September, transferred with two other men to the 3rd Reinforcement Depot under Special Orders 71 of the battalion. He sailed a month after that. The last weeks of his service were not spent with the unit this record follows.

His discharge is dated 22 October 1945, at the Separation Center, Indiantown Gap, Pennsylvania: one year, five months and eight days of foreign service. He was thirty-nine, and went home to the farm at RFD 2, Caro, Michigan.
