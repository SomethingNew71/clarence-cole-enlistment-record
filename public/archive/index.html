    How to read a morning report

    The upper table lists changes in status — a man moving from duty to hospital, a promotion, a transfer — each with a serial number, name, grade, and a code. The block below it records the unit's strength that day. The handwritten paragraph in the middle, Record of Events, is the only free text on the form, and it is where the war shows up.

    Checking the serial numbers

    The Archives hold a punch card for nearly every man who entered the Army between 1938 and 1946, keyed on his serial number. Every serial read off this film was looked up in it. Most land on a card bearing the same name. Sixty-two do not, and for those the man himself is on a card one or two digits away — which says which digit was misread. The same serials were checked against the Archives' record of American prisoners of war, 143,374 of them; none of the 524 appears in it. None of this has been applied: the card file has an error rate of its own, and a sixth of the cards were lost before the Archives converted them, so a missing card means nothing. The findings are held as questions to put back to the film.

    The battalion's own records

    The battalion kept records of its own, and they survive at the National Archives at College Park. They have not been read. A unit history running from 15 November 1942 to November 1945; after-action reports for June 1944 and the summer of 1945; general orders for 1943 to 1945. All three are filed as FABN-153 and sit in one box, 15969. None of it is digitised. The two questions this site leaves open — the calibre of the guns, and the number and date of the Bronze Star order — should be answered there.

    On uncertainty

    These are carbon copies of pencil, photographed onto microfilm eighty years ago. Where a reading is uncertain it is marked as such rather than smoothed over.

    On the weather

    The cards never mention the weather. The word does not appear in any of the 284 frames. The line marked modelled on each card is therefore not from the film: it is ERA5, a modern weather model rerun over the observations that survive from the 1940s, read for the day and the place the card names.

    It is regional, not local. ERA5 reports a grid cell roughly 25 km across, and the cell used can sit up to 16 km from the village in the station entry. Days whose station does not resolve to a place get no weather rather than the weather of the day before, and the Atlantic crossing gets none at all — the battery was aboard a moving ship and one fixed mid-ocean coordinate would not describe it.
